Why fictional characters make us think.

Five animated characters offer unexpected lessons about skepticism, power, ego, growing up, and disillusionment.

BY FAVOURLONG-FORM REFLECTIONEDITABLE DRAFT / VERSION 01

Why can an animated character make us laugh one moment and question society the next? Cartoons exaggerate personalities and situations, but their stories can still reveal recognizable patterns in how people think, behave, and treat one another.

01 / Cartoons as thought experiments

Fiction does not have to be realistic to be meaningful. A ridiculous character can represent a familiar attitude, while an absurd situation can expose something troubling about an ordinary institution. Animation makes these contrasts especially visible through satire, facial expressions, and consequences that would be impossible in real life.

Five characters offer different ways to think critically: Huey Freeman, Ed Wuncler Sr., Master Shake, Gumball Watterson, and Squidward Tentacles. None provides a complete theory of human nature. Instead, each creates an opportunity to ask a better question.

02 / Huey Freeman: intelligence, skepticism, and isolation

In The Boondocks, Huey Freeman is politically aware, observant, and often distrustful of the society around him. He notices contradictions that others dismiss, and his refusal to accept easy explanations makes him an interesting example of critical thinking.

Yet being perceptive does not mean being correct about everything. Huey can sound so certain of his conclusions that people stop listening. His seriousness and frustration also create distance between him and others. Through Huey, we can ask whether skepticism helps us understand injustice or sometimes becomes a habit of assuming the worst.

The lesson is not to copy his cynicism. It is to value reading, evidence, historical awareness, and intellectual independence while remembering that curiosity should leave room for disagreement and revision.

03 / Ed Wuncler Sr.: wealth, power, and exploitation

Ed Wuncler Sr. represents a very different perspective in The Boondocks. As a wealthy and influential Woodcrest businessman, he shows how power can be disguised as generosity or ordinary business.

In the episode The Block Is Hot, he takes over Jazmine's lemonade stand with the promise of a pony. The apparent opportunity becomes an exploitative arrangement: Jazmine works hard, while contractual terms and expenses keep her from receiving what she expected. Wuncler's confident business language does not make the relationship fair.

The episode invites questions about unequal bargaining power, hidden costs, and whether an agreement can be technically arranged yet still deeply unjust. It also connects to Huey's warnings: seeing a problem is important, but persuading others to recognize it can be difficult.

Wuncler is a satirical character, not proof that every wealthy person behaves the same way. The useful question is how institutions should prevent people with less power from being taken advantage of.

04 / Master Shake: ego without responsibility

Master Shake from Aqua Teen Hunger Force is almost the opposite of a thoughtful critic. He declares himself important, frequently makes selfish decisions, and mistreats Meatwad while expecting others—especially Frylock—to deal with the consequences.

The show's absurd humor magnifies traits that are recognizable outside a cartoon: overconfidence, entitlement, lack of empathy, and the urge to blame someone else. Shake often wants the status of leadership without the responsibility that comes with it.

Watching him raises a simple but valuable distinction: confidence is not competence. Speaking loudly, claiming authority, or acting superior does not automatically make someone wise. We can understand the joke without diagnosing the character or excusing how he treats others.

05 / Gumball Watterson: impulsiveness and growing up

In The Amazing World of Gumball, Gumball Watterson can be sarcastic, mischievous, dramatic, and quick to act before thinking. His schemes create chaos, but he can also be loyal and caring toward the people close to him.

That combination makes him useful for thinking about adolescence. Being clever is different from having good judgment. A person can notice what is wrong with everyone else and still misunderstand their own motives, especially when embarrassment, pride, or jealousy takes over.

Gumball's adventures ask us to separate intention from impact. Trying to help or win an argument does not guarantee a good outcome. What matters is whether someone can recognize the consequences, apologize, and grow.

06 / Squidward: frustration, creativity, and burnout

Squidward Tentacles from SpongeBob SquarePants works as a cashier at the Krusty Krab but would rather devote himself to music, painting, and a quieter life. He is often sarcastic, irritated, and convinced that the people around him fail to appreciate his talents.

It is easy to laugh at his complaints. Yet viewers can also recognize the gap between his ambitions and his daily routine. His desire for solitude is understandable when SpongeBob and Patrick repeatedly interrupt his peace, and his workplace is hardly an ideal setting for an aspiring artist.

Still, frustration does not justify treating everyone with contempt. Squidward can be dismissive and unkind, and pride sometimes prevents him from forming meaningful connections. His character raises a question with no simple answer: how do disappointing circumstances shape our attitude, and when are we responsible for changing it?

SIDE NOTE / A DISTINCTION WORTH KEEPING

Explaining why a character acts badly is different from saying that the behavior is acceptable.

07 / Five characters, five different mirrors

These characters have little in common on the surface. One is a young political observer; another is a wealthy businessman; one is a talking milkshake; another is a mischievous blue cat; and the last is an exhausted cashier with artistic dreams. But comparing them reveals useful contrasts.

Huey asks what happens when intelligence becomes cynicism. Wuncler exposes how influence can be used unfairly. Master Shake shows entitlement without accountability. Gumball illustrates the difference between quick thinking and maturity. Squidward makes us consider how unmet ambitions and constant irritation affect our relationships.

None of them should become an unquestioned role model. Even a character we strongly relate to can have blind spots. Identifying with someone is a beginning for reflection, not a reason to defend every choice they make.

08 / From entertainment to critical thinking

A stronger way to watch a cartoon is to ask three questions. What does the character do? Look at actions rather than labels. What does the story suggest about those actions? Notice consequences, irony, and the reactions of other characters. How does this connect to the real world? Treat the scene as a starting point for reading and research—not as scientific proof.

It also helps to distinguish a show's satire from a character's personal beliefs. A selfish character may be written to criticize selfishness, not celebrate it. A skeptical character may challenge society while the story simultaneously challenges his certainty. Interpretations should be supported by actual scenes and remain open to other readings.

09 / The questions after the credits

Cartoons do more than entertain because they give us a safe distance from difficult subjects. They can make greed look absurd, reveal the loneliness behind irritation, or show how a clever person still makes mistakes. Their exaggeration invites us to notice patterns that everyday life can hide.

Maybe the most valuable habit is not asking, “Which character am I?” but asking, “What can I learn from this character's choices—and what should I question?” That is where entertainment becomes reflection.
